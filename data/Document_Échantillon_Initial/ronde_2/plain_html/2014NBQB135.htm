I.      Introduction

 

 

[1]        The applicant is serving life sentences for two first degree murders, an attempted murder and other related crimes (R v. Wood (1994) 94 C.C.C. (3d) 193 (N.S.C.A.); leave to appeal dismissed [1995] S.C.C.A. No. 41). He has been in prison now for approximately 20 years, longer than he has been on the street, having committed his crimes when 18 years of age. He is eligible for full parole in September 2017.

 

[2]        Through the years his prison security classification has gone from maximum level to medium level and back to maximum level. His most recent security classification review at the Atlantic Institution penitentiary determined that he should remain at maximum level. The applicant is challenging that assessment, seeking a habeas corpus order by way of Rule 69.1 of the Rules of Court.  By an amendment at the outset of the hearing, the application also includes the relief of “certiorari in aid” so as to formally bring the record upon which that assessment was made before the Court.  This has been done.

 

[3]        Habeas corpus relief is denied.  These are my reasons.

 

II.    Analysis

 

A.   Legal Principles

 

[4]        The writ of habeas corpus, one of the oldest prerogative writs, has been described as “the birthright of the people” (R.J. Sharpe, The Law of Habeas Corpus, Oxford University Press 1976 at p. 56, footnote 4, citing Wilmot J., Opinion, Wilm. 81 at 83); “probably [of] the greatest constitutional importance of all the prerogative remedies” (Jones and de Villars, Principles of Administrative Law, 2nd Ed., Carswell at p. 476). Actually, it has been called the “Great Writ of Liberty” (See: May v. Ferndale Institution 2005 SCC 82 at para. 19).

 

[5]        The most recent Supreme Court authority addressing habeas corpus relief in the context of a prison setting is Mission Institution v. Khela 2014 SCC 24. The Court reiterated that:

 

… relief in the form of habeas corpus is available in a provincial superior court to an inmate whose “residual liberty” has been reduced by a decision of the prison authorities, and that this relief is distinct from a possible decision to release the inmate entirely from the correctional system … Decisions which might affect an offender’s residual liberty include, but are not limited to, administrative segregation, confinement in a special handling unit and, as in the case at bar, a transfer to a higher security institution.

 

                                                                                                (Ibid. at para. 34)

 

[6]        The Court explained that the test to be applied for habeas corpus is divided into discrete steps:

 

To be successful, an application for habeas corpus must satisfy the following criteria. First, the applicant must establish that he or she has been deprived of liberty. Once a deprivation of liberty is proven, the applicant must raise a legitimate ground upon which to question its legality. If the applicant has raised such a ground, the onus shifts to the respondent authorities to show that the deprivation of liberty was lawful …

 

                                                                                                (Ibid at para. 30)

 

[7]        The real precedential significance of Mission Instituion v. Khela, though, is that the Court expanded the grounds upon which habeas corpus could be invoked. The Supreme Court determined that not only is habeas corpus available to challenge the lawfulness of a detention on the grounds that the “decision maker lacks jurisdiction to order the deprivation of liberty or if there has been a breach of procedural fairness”, it is also now available to assess a decision’s “reasonableness”:

 

… jurisdiction is one requirement to be met for a decision to be lawful. On its own, however, this requirement is not sufficient to make a decision lawful. A decision that is within the decision maker’s jurisdiction but that lacks the safeguards of procedural fairness will not be lawful. Likewise, a decision that lacks an evidentiary foundation or that is arbitrary or unreasonable cannot be lawful, regardless of whether the decision maker had jurisdiction to make it.

 

                                                                                                (Ibid at para. 67)

 

[8]        The Supreme Court went on to import the deferential “Dunsmuir” [Dunsmuir v. New Brunswick 2008 SCC 9] concept of “reasonableness” of decision for that assessment purpose:

 

A … decision that does not fall within the “range of possible, acceptable outcomes which are defensible in respect of the facts and law” will be unlawful … Similarly, a decision that lacks “justification, transparency and intelligibility” will be unlawful … For it to be lawful, the reasons for and the record of the decision must “in fact or in principle support the conclusion reached” …

 

As things stand, a decision will be unreasonable, and therefore unlawful, if an inmate’s liberty interests are sacrificed absent any evidence or on the basis of unreliable or irrelevant evidence, or evidence that cannot support the conclusion, although I do not foreclose the possibility that it may also be unreasonable on other grounds. Deference will be shown to a determination that evidence is reliable, but the authorities will nonetheless have to explain that determination.

 

                                    (Mission Institution v. Khela, supra at paras. 73, 74)

 

[9]        It should be added that the Supreme Court also reiterated that a provincial superior court has no discretion (beyond very limited situations not relevant here) to decline to embark on an inquiry if the applicant “raises a legitimate ground upon which to question the legality of the deprivation” (at para. 41). This was underscored by the Court because a prisoner has an alternative route of complaint in circumstances such as these - to the Federal Court by way of ‘judicial review’ (i.e. for certiorari, prohibition, mandamus, quo warranto or for an injunction or declaratory relief); the Federal Court having exclusive jurisdiction over ‘judicial review’[1] involving federal administrative decisions. In other words, a provincial superior court justice cannot defer to that process; it is, according to the Supreme Court, the prisoner’s choice as to the route to follow, habeas corpus being within the sole jurisdiction of the provincial superior court in regard to federal inmates.  

 

[10]      I need mention this because the alternative redress of ‘judicial review’ before the Federal Court otherwise available to the applicant nonetheless remains relevant to part of my analysis, as will be later explained.

 

B.   Application of the Test for Habeas Corpus

 

[11]      As seen, there are three analytical steps involved when relief by way of habeas corpus is sought:

 

i.              The applicant must establish that he has been deprived of liberty; if so

 

ii.             The applicant must raise a legitimate ground upon which to question the legality of the deprivation of liberty; if so

 

iii.            The respondent must show that the deprivation of liberty is lawful.

 

I will address each in turn.

 

i.              Has a deprivation of liberty been established?

 

[12]      The legal notion of deprivation within prison walls of a prisoner’s residual liberty interests is explained in R v. Miller:

 

… Confinement in a special handling unit or in administrative segregation … is a form of detention that is distinct and separate from that imposed on the general inmate population. It involves a significant reduction in the residual liberty of the inmate. It is in fact a new detention of the inmate, purporting to rest on its own foundation of legal authority. It is that particular form of detention or deprivation of liberty which is the object of the challenge by habeas corpus. It is release from that form of detention that is sought. For the reasons indicated above, I can see no sound reason in principle, having to do with the nature and role of habeas corpus, why habeas corpus should not be available for that purpose. I do not say that habeas corpus should lie to challenge any and all conditions of confinement in a penitentiary or prison, including the loss of any privilege enjoyed by the general inmate population. But it should lie in my opinion to challenge the validity of a distinct form of confinement or detention in which the actual physical constraint or deprivation of liberty, as distinct from mere loss of certain privileges, is more restrictive or severe than the normal one in an institution.

 

                                                                                    (Emphasis added)

 

([1985] 2 S.C.R. 613 at para. 36)

 

 

[13]      In most applications for habeas corpus involving “residual liberty interests” this step is easily determined, as for example where, as noted before, a prisoner is administratively segregated from the general population, or confined to a special handling unit; or a prisoner is transferred to a higher security institution as a result of a reassessment of the person’s security classification; for example, from minimum to medium as in May v. Ferndale Institution, supra or medium to maximum as in Mission Institution v. Khela, supra, and, an even more recent example, as in Richards v. Springhill Institution 2014 NSSC 121. In the present case, however, the determination of whether there is a deprivation of the applicant’s residual liberty interest contemplated by habeas corpus is not clear cut because the respondent does not concede the point. As noted at the outset, in this case the applicant’s security classification did not change as a result of the reassessment; it remained at the maximum level.

 

[14]      Before proceeding further with this aspect of the required analysis it is helpful to provide an overview of the applicant’s various security classifications over the last 20 years. In the applicant’s circumstances these assessments are required to be made at least every two years; or they may be done if a specific cause exists, as for example if a transfer to another prison is requested or is being considered.

 

[15]      Upon his convictions and sentences in 1993 the applicant was housed at the Atlantic Provinces maximum security penitentiary at Renous, New Brunswick, known as the Atlantic Institution (AI). Sometime in 1999 his classification changed to medium security, despite having stabbed another prisoner in 1998, and he was transferred to the Leclerc Institution in the Province of Quebec. In 2002 he was transferred to the Collins Bay Institution in Ontario, another medium security prison, and then in 2006 to Drumheller Institution in Alberta, also a medium security prison.

 

[16]      However, commencing in 2006 his security classifications have been at the maximum level, having been then housed in the maximum security penitentiaries in Edmonton, Saskatchewan and at Port-Cartier Institution, Quebec. In that time he committed “violent assaults” on correctional officers, the last time on two officers while at Port-Cartier, resulting in his transfer in 2008 to the Special Handling Unit (SHU) in Quebec. He remained in that Unit for approximately three years, the applicant testifying that the reason he was there so long was because he refused to voluntarily leave.

 

[17]      The applicant was eventually voluntarily transferred to Kingston Penitentiary in August 2011. While there a security reclassification review was completed in March 2012. It confirmed his security level at maximum.  Apparently, the applicant has made a ‘judicial review’ application before the Federal Court challenging that decision. It has either not yet been heard or decided. In any event, because of the pending closure of that prison the applicant was involuntarily transferred to the Atlantic Institution in August 2013. He refused to integrate into the general population and was therefore placed in segregation. Actually, for all intents and purposes, since leaving the Special Handling Unit the applicant has remained in segregation, mostly his choices. Indeed, he has been in segregation 20 months of the last two years.

 

[18]      Because the applicant requested a transfer out to a specific institution and, concurrently, the Atlantic Institution was considering transferring the applicant to an institution to alleviate his segregation status (i.e. in the hopes he will be able to be reintegrated into the general population elsewhere), his then institutional parole officer at Atlantic Institution conducted a reassessment of the applicant’s security classification. It was decided that the applicant’s classification remain at the maximum level (at the time of hearing no transfer had been made).

 

[19]      This time, because of the more recent decision of Mission Institution v. Khela, supra, the applicant brought an application for habeas corpus directly to this Court challenging that security classification. He believes he should be at the medium security level.

 

[20]      I return to the threshold argument raised by the respondent; that the continuation of the applicant’s security classification at the same level of maximum does not amount to a deprivation of the applicant’s residual liberty interest for purposes of habeas corpus. Counsel for the respondent fairly put before me conflicting authorities on the point. The issue was succinctly framed in Moulton v. Canada (Attorney General):

 

… is it possible for initial security classifications, periodic confirmations of inmates’ existing security classifications, or denials of voluntary transfer requests to lower security institutions, to be considered unlawful deprivations of residual liberty capable of invoking a superior court’s habeas corpus jurisdiction?

 

(2010 ONSC 2448, Graham J. at para. 78)

 

[21]      The following comments seem logically persuasive, made in another case that is also representative of the philosophical camp that holds that in circumstances such as in the situation here there is a reduction in an inmate’s residual liberty sufficient to surmount the first step for habeas corpus:

 

… The liberty of the individual is in no way less affected in a situation where the individual has a security classification as maximum and is entitled to a medium or lesser security classification and does not receive it as in the reverse situation if the individual were to have, currently, a medium security classification and that is changed to maximum security classification. If the argument of the Respondent were to be accepted, then an individual who was incarcerated, at any classification level, and was entitled to a lower classification or perhaps to release, for example, by a change in the law, the individual would have no resort to the Writ of Habeas Corpus as there was no further deprivation of his liberty. That proposition, in my view, is incorrect.

 

(A.G. of Canada v. Hollinger [2007] O.J. No. 3326 (S.C.J.) Panet J. at para. 16; See also: Musitano v. Canada (A.G.) [2006] O.J. No. 1152; R v. Scarella [2009] O.J. No. 2667 (Ont. S.C.J.); Hutchinson v. A.G. of Canada 2010 ONSC 535; Bonamy v. Commissioner of Correction Service Canada & Warden of Saskatchewan Penitentiary 2000 SKQB 385).

 

 

[22]      In summary, these cases stand for the proposition that a provincial superior court “has jurisdiction by habeas corpus to make remedial orders in circumstances where the confinement or detention of a prisoner is more restrictive or severe than those contemplated by the Act” (Bonamy v. Commissioner of Correction Service of Canada & Warden of Saskatchewan Penitentiary, supra at para. 17), regardless of the status of that prisoner at the time of the impugned decision. These cases would also buttress their position by maintaining that any other view would be “a ‘static, narrow and formalistic’ interpretation of the scope of habeas corpus [which] would be inconsistent with the ‘grand purpose’ of the ‘Great Writ of Liberty’ given that the ‘rule of law must run within penitentiary walls’” (See: Moulton v. Canada (A.G.), supra at para. 84)

 

[23]      However, on the other side there is another camp of cases that require more to invoke the reach of habeas corpus (See: MacDonald v. Canada (Attorney General) 2007 O.J. No. 418; Holland v. Canada (A.G.) 2013 QCCS 5317; Robinson v. A.G. of Canada 2013 ONSC 7992; See also: Storry v. Williams Head Institution (1997) 139 F.T.R. 122 at para. 5)). In Robinson v. A.G. of Canada, supra, which involved an annual security classification reassessment that continued the inmate’s security level at medium, the learned justice relied on the rationale in two cases to conclude that the decision did not amount to a deprivation of residual liberty for the purpose of a habeas corpus application. One of the cases relied upon was from the Supreme Court, a case dealing with parole (Dumas v. Leclerc Institute [1986] 2 S.C.R. 459), and the other case dealt with the results of a correctional plan that influenced a denial of parole and a denial of a reclassification to a minimum security level (Moldovan v. Canada (A.G) 2012 ONSC 2682).

 

[24]      Healey J. wrote in Robinson v. A.G. of Canada that:

 

In Dumas v. Leclerc Institute [1986] 2 S.C.R. 459 [“Dumas”], at para. 11, Lamer, J. described that there are three different forms of deprivations of liberty in a correctional context:  (1)  the initial deprivation of liberty of imprisonment;  (2)  a substantial change in conditions amounting to a deprivation of residual liberty;  and (3)  a continuation of a deprivation of liberty. This application concerns the third type described in Dumas, because there has been no change in the degree, nature or kind of the applicant’s deprivation of liberty. Habeas corpus was not available to the offender in Dumas because the court found that there was no substantial change in the conditions of the detention. Mr. Dumas had received a decision that he would be released on day parole, but before that parole was actually granted, the parole board reversed its decision. Because he never became a parolee, there was no change in his deprivation of liberty and the SCC found that he had no right of habeas corpus. Lamer, J. indicated at para. 14 that the lawfulness of the decision to rescind parole could be challenged by other means, such as an application under s. 18 of the Federal Court Act, R.S.C. 1970 (2nd Supp.), c.10 or s. 24(1) of the Charter.

 

Dumas was applied in Moldovan v. Canada (Attorney General), 2012 ONSC 2682 [“Moldovan”] to reach a finding that habeas corpus was not available on facts very similar to those on this application.  Mr. Moldovan objected to the fact that his correction plan required sexual offender programming, which he stated contravened the provision of the CCRA, infringed principles of fundamental justice contrary to s. 7 of the Charter and was an abuse of process.  He submitted that his unwillingness to comply with the correction plan, which he believed to be unconstitutional, was taken into account by the National Parole Board in denying him full parole and was one of the factors preventing him from being classified as a minimum-security risk and from being transferred to the minimum-security facility at Beaver Creek.  At para. 15 MacDonnell J. held:

 

  (i) Insofar as the application for habeas corpus is based on the refusal of the applicant’s request to be transferred to a minimum-security facility, it is dismissed.  There has been no change in his security classification, the manner in which a sentence is being served, or the location where it is being served and accordingly there has been no deprivation of liberty that is reviewable by way of habeas corpus…

 

(ii)  Decisions of CSC in relation to correction plans for inmates do not constitute deprivations of liberty and are not reviewable by way of habeas corpus.   They are reviewable under the  scheme laid down in the CCRA, but by reason of ss. 18 of the Federal Courts Act, 1985, R.S.C., c.F-7, exclusive jurisdiction for judicial review in that respect is vested in the Federal Court.

 

Dumas and Moldovan are the complete answer to this application. The applicant challenges the assessment tools and information given to the decision-maker, and objects to the requirement of his correctional plan that he disassociates with Hells Angels. There has been no deprivation of his liberty because his status remains unchanged. Further, the decisions made by CSC in this case, and the criteria on which they are based, are only challengeable in the Federal Court.

 

In addition, the applicant has raised the constitutionality of the correction plan and/or the Assessment for Decision by arguing that it infringes his right to freedom of association under s. 2(d) of the Charter because he is expected to not associate with members of Hells Angels.  The Supreme court has made it clear that habeas corpus is directed at liberty interests only, corresponding to s. 7 and s. 9 of the Charter:  May v. Ferndale, supra, at para. 22 (S.C.C.);  R v. Miller,  [1985] 2 S.C.R. 613 at para. 36.  Accordingly, the argument made by the applicant is not available to him within a habeas corpus application.

 

The applicant relies on several decisions of this court in support of his proposition that the refusal to reclassify him so as to support a transfer to a minimum-security facility is a deprivation of liberty. These decisions are Musitano v. Canada (Attorney General), [2006]  O.J. No. 1152 (S.C.J.);  Canada v. Hollinger, [2007] O.J. No. 3326 (S.C.J.); and Her Majesty the Queen v. Scarcella, 2009, CanLII 32918 (Ont. S.C.). None of the these decisions makes reference to Dumas, and in particular  the clear direction set out in Dumas that where there has been no change in the conditions or circumstances of detention, no deprivation of liberty occurs so as to entitle the offender to apply for habeas corpus. For this reason, I disagree with the submission of the applicant’s counsel that Musitano, Hollinger and Scarcella can be relied upon to conclude that the decision to grant a lesser security classification to facilitate transfer from a more restrictive institutional setting amounts to a deprivation of residual liberty.

 

                                                                                                (Emphasis added)

 

 

[25]      I would align myself with Robinson v. A.G. of Canada, supra. However, my own pathway to decision may be different. I will endeavor to explain.

 

[26]      The emphasis placed by the other philosophical camp on the rule of law running within penitentiary walls actually raises a very basic question underpinning the “rule of law” - the jurisdiction of a judge; for at the heart of the rule of law is the necessary limitation that judges can only exercise the authority given to it by the people. Given that provincial superior courts clearly do have jurisdiction to entertain habeas corpus, this begs the question faced here as to when habeas corpus can be used. First of all, the rule of law does not only run inside prison walls by way of habeas corpus. As we have seen, it is just that a provincial superior court cannot issue the prerogative writs of certiorari, prohibition, mandamus, or quo warranto; nor can it grant an injunction or make a declaration involving federal inmates. All those remedies have been reserved exclusively to the Federal Court through the Federal Courts Act (Mission Institution v. Khela, supra).

 

[27]      Although the Supreme Court in Khela expanded the scope of habeas corpus by adding “reasonableness” to the grounds upon which a decision can be reviewed for “unlawfulness”, the Court did not expand or otherwise change the historic remedy available by way of habeas corpus should there be a finding of “unlawfulness”. The remedy is a statement to that effect and a corresponding “release order”; i.e., an order directing the authorities to place the inmate in the position he or she would be but for the unlawful restriction on outright liberty or the reduction of residual liberty:

 

… But, all a provincial superior court can do is determine that the detention is unlawful and then rule on a motion for discharge.

 

(Mission Institution v. Khela, supra at para. 39; See e.g. Bradley v. Canada (A.G.) 2012 NSSC 173 at para. 78-79)

 

[28]      It bears repeating that a provincial superior court issuing a habeas corpus order is not quashing nor can it quash the impugned decision and direct a re-consideration by the correctional authorities (certiorari) or make an independent decision as to the proper security classification level (certiorari) or compel the authorities to make a decision (mandamus) or otherwise make a declaration (declaratory order) or stop the correctional authorities from taking such other steps as they decide over the inmate (injunction).

 

[29]      The stark reality a court faces in the situation of an inmate whose security classification level has not changed is that a judge could only “release” the inmate, should there be a finding of “unlawfulness” of decision, back to the same level of security (i.e. back to an equivalent institution) that the inmate complains about, in this case maximum security. There would be no power to do otherwise. The answer to the question this fact raises comes full circle – is the kind of potential restriction of residual liberty alleged where there has been no change, one for which habeas corpus is truly available? Or, is habeas corpus employed in such circumstance in reality a provincial superior court judge exercising the ‘judicial review’ jurisdiction of a federal court judge – figuratively cloaked to look like habeas corpus?

 

[30]      Actually, in the context of this case the applicant’s pleadings underscore what I perceive to be a legal façade. In his “Notice of Preliminary Motion for A Habeas Corpus Order” he specifically asked that the “Security Reclassification decision be quashed and [he] be returned to medium”. The Court simply has no power to do either by way of habeas corpus here. Nor, again, should it be overlooked that the expansion of the scope for review under habeas corpus brought about by the Supreme Court in Mission Institution v. Khela, supra, did not bring with it an expansion or extension of the remedy that historically attaches to the writ. Those kinds of expanded remedies are found only in the exclusive jurisdiction of the Federal Court by dictate of statute (Federal Courts Act, ss. 18, 18.1).

 

[31]      The Supreme Court has stressed the importance of ensuring the availability and accessibility of the writ of habeas corpus over concerns of concurrent or conflicting jurisdictions (See: Mission Institution v. Khela, supra at paras. 33, 34). Indeed, the Supreme Court’s decision in Khela exemplifies that point. However, the fact that the validity of a particular form of confinement within a prison can be now reviewed by habeas corpus in like manner to another prerogative writ (certiorari), does not change the gravamen of the concern here, that the writ of habeas corpus cannot by remedy functionally reach certain alleged reductions or restrictions in residual liberty – the continuation of the same security classification being one. This is, hopefully, not a concern on my part over concurrent or conflicting jurisdiction but, rather, a real concern over usurpation of jurisdiction.

 

[32]      To underscore the point, it is necessary to reiterate that in the circumstances here, if this Court were to find the respondent’s decision “unlawful”, no order for release can be effectively made since the applicant would still remain in maximum security. This is contrary to the writ’s raison d’être - the very purpose or object of habeas corpus. Indeed, it would be opposite the reason why the Supreme Court extended the reach of habeas corpus to protect residual liberty interests of inmates in the first place:

 

…It is that particular form of detention or deprivation of liberty which is the object of the challenge by habeas corpus. It is release from that form of detention that is sought. For the reason indicated above, I can see no sound reason in principle, having to do with the nature and role of habeas corpus, why habeas corpus should not be available for that purpose. I do not say that habeas corpus should lie to challenge any and all conditions of confinement in a penitentiary or prison …

 

                                                                                                (Emphasis added)

 

(R v. Miller, supra at para. 36)

 

 

[33]      In circumstances such as these should a decision be considered “unlawful”, what is left to a provincial superior court justice is to make a statement to that effect, actually a declaratory order. I recognize that this too appears logically attractive, except habeas corpus is by its very nature a “remedial” remedy, not a “declaratory” one: “While habeas corpus is declaratory in that it does not quash an impugned decision (in fact it may often be used to secure a declaration as to status), it does result in an order of release which can be directly enforced” (Sharpe, The law of Habeas Corpus, supra at pp. 58-59). On the other hand, a declaratory order is one that can go no further than confirm or deny a legal right without consequential relief (See: Lazar Sarna, The Law of Declaratory Judgments, 2007 (3rd Ed.), Thomson/Carswell, at p. 1). As malleable as habeas corpus has been shown to be, in my opinion the very nature of the writ would be transformed by judicial fiat if employed in that limited manner, at least when set in the wider context of a statutory framework that unequivocally reserves exclusive jurisdiction to the Federal Court to entertain and issue declaratory orders over decisions made in regard to federal inmates (See e.g. Canada v. Solosky [1980] 1 S.C.R. 821).

 

[34]      As uncomfortable as it is to engage in a line of reasoning that might be perceived by some to be a “static, narrow and formulistic” interpretation of the “Great Writ” of habeas corpus, I would be even more uncomfortable in applying, for pragmatic reasons, another prerogative writ in disguise or other remedy in disguise, over which I had no jurisdiction in law; that line having been drawn by Parliament.

 

[35]      In my view, the security classification review decision made here that continued the same level of security over the applicant is not a deprivation of residual liberty for which the remedy of habeas corpus can respond. Put differently, if in a particular circumstance the “Great Writ of Liberty” cannot upon a finding of “unlawfulness” remediate by granting liberty, including greater residual liberty, then ipso facto the nature of the liberty interest alleged to be at stake is not one for which that writ is or can be made available. In arriving at this conclusion I need to stress that the Court is not declining to exercise its habeas corpus jurisdiction, which this Court cannot do except in very narrow situations. Rather, the Court has found that the applicant has not met the first step on habeas corpus “review”, i.e. has not established a reduction or restriction in his residual liberty interests for which habeas corpus is available, and, therefore, the Court has no jurisdiction to proceed further. This result does not mean that the law stops running inside a prison setting; it simply means that it must in certain circumstances run along a different track. In my opinion, the situation here is one such circumstance.

 

[36]      For these reasons, and with great respect for those who hold a different view, I have aligned myself with the cases which have found that a continuation of an existing security classification without more is not sufficient to invoke habeas corpus.

 

[37]      In the event I am wrong in that determination and there is at law a potential restriction or reduction in the applicant’s residual liberty interest that can be reached by habeas corpus, I will continue with the analysis.

 

ii.            Has the applicant raised a legitimate ground?

 

[38]      The main thrust of the applicant’s complaints is that the decision to continue his security classification at the maximum level is not “reasonable”. In theory, though, at least when it comes to challenges to the substantive reasonableness of the impugned decision, the applicant is required to do more than articulate the legal grounds he relies in order to surmount this threshold step. The applicant needs to raise a legitimate doubt as to the reasonableness of the detention before “the provincial superior court judge is required to examine the substance of the decision and determine whether the evidence presented by the detaining authorities is reliable and supports their decision” (See: Khela v. Mission Institution, supra at para. 78). This requirement is actually reflected in Rule 69.1 of the Rules of Court, which provides for a “Notice of Preliminary Motion for a Habeas Corpus Order”. This was in fact the procedure the applicant adopted to bring his request before the Court.

 

[39]      In reality, however, a court cannot normally do one without doing the other, that is, in order to determine whether the applicant has raised a legitimate doubt about the reasonableness of the decision, a court needs to consider the record upon which that decision was made. In such an event, a court is invariably embarking upon a full consideration of reasonableness. In other words, this screening threshold more often than not is more illusionary than real:

 

…While it may be necessary as a practical matter to have an initial screening of the application, it is an important safeguard of liberty that the writ not be refused on discretionary grounds. Plainly, the court is given considerable leeway in deciding whether the application has a sound legal basis, and it has been observed that an element of judicial discretion is necessarily involved at this stage. However, if the prisoner does raise an arguable issue there is no room for discretion: the matter should proceed to hearing so that a full and proper determination can be made. The non-discretionary nature of habeas corpus is an important difference between it and the other prerogative writs.

 

(R.J. Sharpe, The Law of Habeas Corpus, supra at pp. 56-57)

 

 

[40]      In keeping with the modern face of this ancient remedy and mindful of the core constitutional values this prerogative writ protects, I am prepared to assume the applicant has raised a legitimate doubt on the face of his pleadings so as to require a full inquiry and without the requirement of filing any other process. Additionally, the applicant raised a question of procedural fairness in the making of that assessment. This too would warrant an inquiry. In fact, a full hearing was conducted.

 

[41]      For all these reasons the applicant has met the onus to have his claim for relief by way of a writ of habeas corpus adjudged.

 

iii.           Has the respondent shown that the deprivation of the applicant’s residual liberty is lawful?

 

[42]      There is no question that the respondent has the statutory jurisdiction to make the decisions it did over the applicant (see: Corrections and Conditional Release Act, ss. 28, 29). Indeed, it is part of the respondent’s mandate (s. 30).

 

[43]      Rather, the applicant’s complaints are directed at the substantive “reasonableness” of the decision to continue to classify his security level at maximum and, secondarily, at the procedural “fairness” of the manner in which that decision was made. Either finding would, of course, amount to a finding of “unlawfulness”, requiring the remedy of habeas corpus.

 

 

 

a)    Was the decision to classify the applicant’s security level at maximum reasonable?

 

[44]      For security classification review the correctional authorities are required to utilize “a research-based computerized tool” known as a “Security Reclassification Scale” (SRS). It has been succinctly described in one case as follows:

 

The Security Reclassification Scale (“SRS”) is a computer application that provides a security rating based on data entered with respect to various factors related to the assessment of risk: (1) the seriousness of the offence committed by the offender;  (2) the existence of outstanding charges against the offender;  (3) the offender’s performance and behavior while under sentence;  (4)  the offender’s social, criminal and, if applicable, young-offender history;  (5)  any physical or mental illness or disorder suffered;  (6) the offender’s potential for violent behavior;  and (7)  the offender’s continued involvement in criminal activities. The SRS scale has been developed to assist caseworkers to determine the most appropriate level of security at key points throughout the offender’s sentence:  SOP 700-14, at paras. 18-19.

 

The SRS is completed by assigning scores to several factors assessing the offender’s security risk and custody performance. The SRS provides numerical “cut-off levels” which determine a security rating. If the officer completing the review does not agree with the results provided by the SRS, he or she may override the results and give a different security classification. The override provisions are incorporated in the SRS as a means to address factors that may compel the transfer of an offender to a security level that is different from the one obtained through the computer application:  SOP 700-14, at para. 20

 

                                                                                                (Emphasis added)

 

(May v. Ferndale Institution, supra at paras. 102-103)

 

[45]      In the present case, the use of the SRS resulted in a score of 25, commensurate with a medium level security classification only. But, as noted above, the SRS score is not necessarily the determinative factor. Consistent with the above quoted explanation, one document in the record of these proceedings is entitled “Security Reclassification Scale (SRS), Functional Specification, Ver. 4.0.3”. In that document the following directions, among others, are found:

 

…  If the parole officer completing the review does not agree with the results provided by the tool they are to use the override factors and justify why they chose to override the results.

 

…

 

The application generates a score and provides an appropriate security classification of minimum, medium, or maximum. The present scores for each security classification are minimum 0 to 16.5; medium 17.0 to 26.5; maximum 27 to 99.99. If the security classification is calculated within 5% (plus or minus) of the next higher or lower classification, the application displays this information. If you want to select a higher or lower classification than the one generated by the application, enter information in the Override Factor fields.

 

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    (Emphasis in text)

 

(Affidavit of Mark Hare sworn May 1, 2014, Exhibit 2)

 

[46]      On this direction, the applicant’s then institutional parole officer overrode the score obtained, determining that the applicant’s security classification should be at maximum. The applicant’s current institutional parole officer explains in his affidavit how and why that determination was made. Although lengthy, I cannot by summary improve upon the relevant excerpts:

 

7.  In accordance with section 28 of the Corrections and Conditional Release Act, S.C. 1992, c. 20, when a person is confined to a penitentiary, CSC  shall take all reasonable steps to ensure that the penitentiary in which the person is confined to an environment that contains only the necessary restrictions, taking into account:

 

a.     The degree and kind of custody and control necessary for:

 

i.              The safety of the public;

ii.             The safety of the person and other persons in the penitentiary, and

iii.            The security of the penitentiary.

 

b.     Accessibility to:

 

i.              The person’s home community and family;

ii.             A compatible cultural environment;

iii.            A compatible linguistic environment;  and

 

c.    The availability of appropriate programs and services and the person’s willingness to participate in those programs.

 

8.  Section 17 of the Corrections and Conditional Release Regulations provides factors that CSC must take into consideration in determining the security classification to be assigned to an inmate pursuant to section 30 of the Corrections and Conditional Release Act:

 

            (a)  the seriousness of the offence committed by the inmate;

            (b)  any outstanding charges against the inmate;

            (c)  the inmate’s performance and behavior while under sentence;

(d)  the inmate’s social, criminal and, if available, young-offender history and any dangerous offender designation under the Criminal Code;

(e)  any physical or mental illness or disorder suffered by the inmate;

(f)  the inmate’s potential for violent behavior;  and

(g)  the inmate’s continued involvement in criminal activities.

 

9.  As Mr. Wood’s current parole officer, I consider Mr. Wood to have a “high” level of “institutional adjustment” concerns and that this factor has resulted in CSC’s assessment that Mr. Wood will remain classified as a “maximum” security offender at this time.  In CSC’s view, Mr. Wood is currently confined to the environment that provides him with only the necessary restrictions in accordance with section 28 of the Corrections and Conditional Release Act. 

 

10. A finding of “high” with regards to the factor of “institutional adjustment” means that CSC considers Mr. Wood to require a high degree of supervision and control within the penitentiary.  In accordance with section 30 of the Corrections and Conditional Release Act and sections 17 and 18(a)(ii) of the Corrections and Conditional Release Regulations, SOR/92-620, an inmate who requires a high degree of supervision and control within the penitentiary must be classified as a “maximum” security inmate. CSC is mandated to follow the relevant statutory regime set out in the Corrections and Conditional Release Act and the Corrections and Conditional Release Regulations.

 

11.       In assessing security classification in accordance with section 17 of the Corrections and Conditional Release Regulations, CSC officials are also guided by Commissioner’s Directive 710-6, “Review of Offender Security Classification”;  a copy is attached to my Affidavit as Exhibit “6”.  In rating an offender’s institution adjustment as either low, moderate, or high, CSC takes into account the following adjustment factors noted in Annex B of Commissioner’s Directive 710-6 (and any other relevant considerations):

 

Low- The inmate has demonstrated:

 

1.     A pattern of satisfactory institutional adjustment;  no special management intervention is required

2.    The ability and motivation to interact effectively and responsibly with others, individually and in groups, with little or no supervision

3.    Motivation towards self-improvement by actively participating in a Correctional Plan designed to meet his/her dynamic factors, particularly those relating to facilitating his/her reintegration into the community

 

Normal – The inmate has demonstrated:

 

1.     Some difficulties causing moderate institutional adjustment problems and requiring some management intervention

2.    The potential to interact effectively with others, individually and in moderately structured groups, but needs regular and often direct supervision

3.    An interest and active participation in a Correctional Plan designed to meet his/her dynamic factors, particularly those which would lead to a transfer to a less structured environment and ultimately to his/her reintegration into the community

 

High- The inmate has demonstrated:

 

1.    Frequent or major difficulties causing serious institutional adjustment problems and requiring significant/constant management intervention

2.    A requirement for a highly structured environment in which individual or group interaction is subject to constant and direct supervision

3.    An uncooperative attitude toward institutional programs and staff and present a potentially serious management problem within an institution

 

12.  CSC considers that Mr. Wood has demonstrated, and is demonstrating, all three factors listed under the “high” category. Mr. Wood requires significant intervention from his Case Management Team; he needs to have his interpersonal interactions closely monitored to assess his risk to others; and he displays an uncooperative attitude towards program interventions. As an example, CSC does and would need to review Mr. Wood in a highly structured environment in order to consider and assess whether he can be, at some point, managed in a less structured environment without direct and constant supervision.

 

13.       The SRS (see Exhibit “1” (SRS) and Exhibit “2” (Scoring Matrix)) assists CSC with determining the appropriate security classification for individual offenders. The SRS is a research-based computerized tool employed by CSC to assist it in determining the most appropriate level of security at various points throughout an offender’s sentence. The SRS takes into account three primary areas:

           

(a)  Institution Adjustment;

(b)  Escape Risk;  and

(c)  Public Safety.

 

14.  The SRS is completed online by a CSC staff member, usually the offender’s assigned parole officer, who assigns scores to 15 factors that assess the offender’s security risk and in-custody performance (where factors are not fixed/static, the assessor chooses the appropriate response from a drop-down menu).  An overall SRS score is then generated and the assessor must complete an A4D, which may or may not align with the generated score.  In cases where it does not, the A4D must specify why this is the case.  Each final security level assessment must conform to the statutory scheme by setting out a risk analysis under the headings:  institutional adjustment, escape risk, and risk to public safety.

 

15.       Although the SRS and Scoring Matrix tools CSC use in the classification process have scored Mr. Wood as a “medium” offender (with a score of 25) (see Exhibit “1” (SRS)  and Exhibit “2” (Scoring Matrix), the tool does not take into account all factors, and it does not replace the clinical assessment of experienced and specialized CSC staff who may override a computerized score where, in exercising their professional judgment in accordance with the statutory regime, they view Mr. Wood as being properly classified as a “maximum” security inmate.  This is chiefly because of his high institutional adjustment concerns that relate to his negative attitude, institutional adjustment problems, non-compliance with his Correctional Plan, five placements into segregation and past violent incidents against an inmate (repeated stabbing, 1998 at Atlantic Institution) and two CSC officers (2007 and 2008)  (see esp. Exhibit “3” (A4D at pages 2-3)) .

 

16.       The A4D (see Exhibit “3”) sets out the assessor’s reasoning with respect to the security classification review and provides a recommendations and rationale for each of the three security classification headings (institutional adjustment, escape risk, and risk to public safety).  The A4D concludes with a recommendation as to the appropriate security classification for that offender.  The A4D will note, where applicable, whether other members of the offender’s Case Management Team disagree with the assessor’s opinion as to the appropriate security classification.

 

17.  Following completion of the A4D,  a Manager, Assessment and Intervention, submits a  written recommendation to the decision maker (either the Warden or the Warden’s delegate) based on the information contained in the A4D, along with any other information that has come to light since the completion of the A4D, such as any rebuttals submitted by the offender.

 

18.       As per section 18 of the Corrections and Conditional Release Regulations, a finding of high “institutional adjustment” necessarily results in a “maximum” security classification, regardless of the levels assigned to “escape risk” and “public safety”.

 

(Affidavit of Mark Hare sworn May 1, 2014)

 

[47]      Much of the applicant’s argument was directed at the accuracy of the SRS static score and the applicability of the override provision. However, as seen, that score is not necessarily determinative. Indeed, in cross-examination the applicant’s current institutional parole officer, Mark Hare, fairly conceded that if he had been scoring the applicant under that program he would have assigned a lower rating in two categories, effectively resulting in a total score of 23, not 25, which, in any event, would still have placed the applicant at the medium security level. As well, I reject the applicant’s further argument that the discretionary range of plus or minus 5% of the actual score generated (see above) is the only exercise of discretion open to the correctional authorities. Both the evidence of the parole officer, the documentary evidence (see above) and the case law authorities (e.g. Mission Institution v. Khela, supra at paras. 96-97; May v. Ferndale Insitution, supra; Spindler v. Millhaven Institution (2003) 15 C.R. (6th) 183 (Ont. C.A.) at para.5) reveal that there is no such constraint. The override provision can stand alone. Actually, the emphasis on so-called dynamic factors over static factors by the correctional authorities in this case was brought home when the applicant’s current parole officer added (in answer to a direct question put to him by the applicant) that despite that score he too would have overrode the SRS and determined that the applicant’s security classification should remain at the maximum level by reason of “high institutional adjustment” concerns.

 

[48]      And, it is on the assessment of the category of “institutional adjustment” and its singular influence on the decision to continue to classify the applicant’s security classification at maximum that, in my opinion, the issue of reasonableness of decision is to be decided. This is because Section 18 of the Corrections and Conditional Release Regulations (SOR/92- 620) requires that:

 

For the purposes of section 30 of the Act [Corrections and Conditional Release Act], an inmate shall be classified as

 

(a)  Maximum security where the inmate is assessed by the Service as

 

(i)            …

(ii)           requiring a high degree of supervision and control within the penitentiary;

 

(Emphasis added)

 

[49]      In other words, because the respondent relied on their finding that the applicant’s institutional adjustment is high (i.e. the applicant requires a high degree of supervision and control within the penitentiary) as the reason to override the SRS score, then it is the reasonableness of that finding that informs the overall “reasonableness” of the decision to classify him at the maximum security level. In this regard, and as mentioned in the affidavit evidence set out before, the applicant’s then institutional parole officer, Jody Donovan, was required to explain her rationale through a document referred to as an “A4D”.

 

[50]      In that “A4D” report Ms. Donovan specifically addressed, inter alia, her reasons for overriding the SRS score:

 

(b)  Inmate Security Level Review

 

The reader is referred to the Security Reclassification Scale (SRS) completed on 2013.09.24 in conjunction with this report. The numerical score is 25 which is indicative of medium security. I am not in agreement with the score based on his attitude, non-compliance with his Correctional Plan and his periods of segregation which result in an assessment of high for institutional adjustment.

 

Institutional Adjustment – High (no change)

 

Wood’s last OSL was completed on 2012.03.28 resulting in ascore of 27.5 which is indicative of maximum security. The past two years make up this review period. During that time he has been in segregation on 5 separate occasions, 3 of which were involuntary and the remaining 2 were voluntary. The accumulative days in segregation were 442. On a positive note, he had not incurred any charges in the past two years and has only been involved in one incident in which his visitor was denied due to the drug dog indicating on the visitor.

 

Wood arrived at AI on 2013.08.26. Prior to his arrival he wrote letters to both A/MAI and the SIO department stating that he would not integrate at AI and expressing his disdain for the correctional Services of Canada and its employees.

 

The initial interview took place with A/MAI Keith Fowler present as he was familiar with Wood’s case. Wood stated that he had no intention of working with his CMT and did not care to know who his parole officer was. He was clear during the interview that he would not consider integrating at AI and that he will get transferred to the Quebec Region one way or another.

 

Since the initial meeting Wood has written letters to his CMT referring to himself as “you better” and referring to his CMT as “Maggots”. In these letters he states that the question is not if he will ever leave segregation the question is whether he will stay or go to the SHU. During a subsequent interview he stated that he was not happy that he was not on the recent transfer. As he just arrived at AI on 2013.08.26 I informed him that there was no way that he would be processed for a transfer merely weeks following his arrival. He proceeded to state that he will be on the next transfer one way or another. Following this statement he walked away from the common room to his range and stated “do something protective with your life and take it”. This type of behavior towards his CMT is not new, as his files are plagued with the same statements and references throughout the years. He continues to demonstrate that he is unwilling to work effectively with his CMT and therefore makes his case difficult to manage.

 

He has made his position quit [sp.?] clear, and given his past history of assaultive behavior towards both staff and other inmates he was segregated following the orientation process.

 

There are no viable reintegration plans at AI for Wood as he will not entertain the idea of remaining here and thus a transfer is necessary to alleviate his segregation status. Wood has not been able to demonstrate a period of stability within an Open Population to warrant a reduction in this area.

 

Based on the aforementioned information his institutional adjustment is being maintained as high.

 

[51]      The reliability or accuracy of the evidence relied upon by Ms. Donovan to base her conclusion has not been undermined by the applicant. Actually, in many ways he has confirmed it. His position is that he “stopped dealing with CSC”, stopped cooperating over the last number of years because they were not treating him fairly; that CSC was not acting in good faith towards him. It is his repeatedly stated view that his “case plan is complete” and that, consequently, CSC’s repeated requests that he embark on additional programming is not warranted. The applicant registers his protests to what he perceives to be unfair treatment by walking away, so to speak; as the applicant put it: “I told CSC see you later”. This protest takes the form of his voluntary segregations or extensions of segregations, and is reflected in the derogatory manner he communicates with staff, either verbally or by letter, and in his refusal to accept CSC direction in regards to case planning. I should add that the evidence also reveals that the applicant attempts through veiled threats to dictate the institution in which he is to be housed. The consequence of all of this is a figurative merry-go round by CSC staff with him.

 

[52]      The applicant points out that over the last number of years he has had no institutional charges or incidents; that he has no substance abuse issues; that he is not associated with any gangs; and otherwise does not flaunt or manipulate the institutional rules. He simply refuses to cooperate with CSC in his case planning. The applicant’s seeming ingrained attitude over the last number of years is spotlighted by the fact that since the last security classification review the applicant has been assigned a new institutional parole officer, Mark Hare. The evidence on the hearing is that Mr. Hare was the applicant’s parole officer for a period in the 1990’s and he may be one of the few, if not the only one, that the applicant trusts. Only time will tell if Mr. Hare can make a difference.

 

[53]      The applicant’s position can be distilled down to this: In all of these circumstances it is unreasonable for CSC to find that his institutional adjustment is of high concern, resulting in his security level classification remaining at maximum.

 

[54]      However, a wider, less myopic perspective than the applicant’s is required to properly assess the reasonableness of the respondent’s decision as set out in the “A4D” report. In my view, any assessment must begin with the predicate offences for which the applicant is serving his sentences. These crimes are, by any measure, heinous; coldly executed murders and an attempted murder of his co-workers in a fast food restaurant in the course of a robbery with two accomplices. This is the prism through which the decision of the correctional authorities needs to be assessed and on the record before me was viewed by them. Against the backdrop of these inexplicable crimes is the evidence that despite violence programming in 2004 the applicant went on to commit assaults on prison employees and since sometime in 2008 (beginning with his transfer to the Special Handling Unit (SHU) in Quebec) he has, except for very brief forays, been isolated from the general prison population up to the present time. Indeed, he is presently segregated, refusing to integrate. There is also the evidence of his assault on another inmate back in 1998. Despite this background the applicant maintains that the respondent should have reclassified him at medium security, without more.

 

[55]      When all of this is coupled to his longstanding attitude that his “case plan” is complete, I can come to no other conclusion but that deference is owed to the finding of a “high institutional adjustment”, thereby overriding the SRS score. That finding was not arbitrary and is soundly supported by the evidence relied upon by the respondent and falls well within the “range of possible, acceptable outcomes which are defensible in respect of the facts and law”. As the Supreme Court reminded us:

 

… Determining whether an inmate poses a threat to the security of the penitentiary or of the individuals who live and work in it requires intimate knowledge of that penitentiary’s culture and of the behavior of the individuals inside its walls. Wardens and the Commissioner possess this knowledge, and related practical experience, to a greater degree than a provincial superior court judge.

 

(Mission Institution v. Khela, supra at para. 76)

 

[56]      Consequently, I find the decision to classify the applicant’s security level at maximum to be reasonable, in keeping with the “least restrictive principle” to be applied in deciding an inmate’s placement as required by the Corrections and Conditional Release Act, s. 28.  Indeed, in those circumstances a maximum security classification is mandated by the statute (Corrections and Conditional Release Act, s. 30; Corrections and Conditional Regulations, s. 18).

 

[57]      The respondent’s decision was “justifiable, transparent and intelligible”. It was eminently reasonable for the respondent to believe, and was clearly expressed and supported by them, that the applicant requires “a high degree of supervision and control within the penitentiary”. By his attitude and actions the applicant has not given the respondent any opportunity to make any other determination. Quite frankly, reliance by the respondent on the SRS score alone would have amounted to nothing more than a ‘watching brief’ on the respondent’s part (i.e. see what happens at the medium security level), which, conversely in my estimation, perhaps may not have fallen within the range of possible, acceptable outcomes of decision making by the respondent given their public mandate - their duty to protect others and to rehabilitate the inmate. In any event:

 

The administration of a penal institution is complex, involving considerations of the rights of the prisoners, the safety of inmates and staff and the interests of the general public. The court must be cautious when making an order which affects that administration.

 

(Khela v. Mission Institution, 2011 BCCA 450 at paras 85-86)

 

b)    Was the process or procedure employed by the respondent to arrive at the decision procedurally fair?

 

[58]      The nature of the applicant’s procedural fairness complaint is set out in his application as follows:

 

Though the security decision was finalized 2014/01/06, it was written by the author Jody Donovan on 2013/09/26. Mr. Wood was wholly unaware that said report was being written and was not informed or consulted on it. Mr. Wood found out about the review 2014/01/06 when the final decision was given to him. Inmates serving life sentences are, generally, given security reviews once every two years and, thus, the review (written 2013/09/26) was not “due” or expected until 2014/03/30. It was prepared six months early and finalized circa 3 months later and at no time was Wood given an opportunity to review the decision before it was finalized. Indeed, he was not even told about it.

 

[59]      Recall that what is being challenged by the applicant is the security classification only (i.e. the security review decision), not any other decision by the respondent. The thrust of the applicant’s complaint appears directed at establishing that there is a statutory or regulatory requirement of prior notice and consultation. At the end of the court hearing I granted an opportunity to the applicant to find and subsequently file any CSC materials or authorities that would support his procedural complaint position, as well as related to the question of liberty interest (previously addressed) and granted the respondent leave to file additional authorities. Post hearing the applicant did file documents and written argument, as well as further written arguments in response to the post hearing filing by the respondent of conflicting case law on the question of residual liberty interest (addressed before) and limited written argument on the question of procedural fairness (All these materials have been marked and included as a Supplemental Record). I saw no need to require further oral submissions.

 

[60]      The rules of natural justice, including the duty of fairness, apply here (See: Cardinal v. Kent Institution [1985] 2 S.C.R. 643 at para. 14). Indeed, as the applicant pointed out, the Corrections and Release Act (CCRA) framework adopts a “Duty to Act Fairly” by the correctional authorities in their dealings with inmates. However, the duty of fairness can demand things at different times:

 

… Both the rules of natural justice and the duty of fairness are variable standards. Their content will depend on the circumstances of the case, the statutory provision and the nature of the matter to be decided …

 

(Knight v. Indian Head School Division No. 19, [1990] 1 S.C.R. 653 at p. 682)

 

This is reflected here. For example, a “Correctional Plan” is required to be developed in consultation with the inmate (CCRA, s. 15.1 (2)), but, obviously given its nature, not so a “Review of Inmate Security Classification”. However, the applicant had a right to written reasons for the classification decision (CCRA, s. 30 (2)) and a right to disclosure of the information considered in the taking of the decision after the decision had been taken (CCRA, s. 27 (2)). (See also: May v. Ferndale Institution, supra at para. 98). The applicant has made no complaints in these regards. Indeed, the disclosure of information requirement enhances procedural fairness:

 

A duty of procedural fairness rests on every public authority making administrative decisions affecting the rights, privileges or interests of an individual … These privileges are reflected in and bolstered by the disclosure requirements imposed by the CCRA.

 

(May v. Ferndale, supra at para. 94)

 

[61]      Commissioner’s Directive 710-6, entitled “Review of Offender Security Classification”, answers the applicant’s complaints that the security classification review could not have been conducted when it was or that it was necessary that he be consulted or advised in advance before the classification decision was made:

 

The decision maker must provide specific ratings for institutional adjustment, escape risk and public safety in every final decision on the inmate’s security level. If the decision maker does not concur with the recommended ratings in the Assessment for Decision, a rationale must be provided for the divergence from the recommendation.

 

The decision maker will provide the inmate with the rationale as well as the information considered in the decision, in writing, within five working days following the review. The inmate will be advised, at the same time, of the right to seek redress using the grievance process pursuant to CD 081-Offender Complaints and Grievances.

 

The Parole Officer/Primary Worker will complete all Security Classification Reviews and Reassessments.

 

A Security Classification Review …will be completed at least once every two years for inmates classified at maximum or medium security level.

 

A review of an inmate’s security classification will be completed prior to making a recommendation for any decision (e.g. transfer, temporary absence, work release or parole).

 

                                                                             

[62]      Applying the required standard of “correctness” (Mission Institution v. Khela, supra at para. 79), there is, in my opinion, no procedural unfairness evident here.

 

III.   Summary

 

[63]      The respondent has shown that the alleged deprivation of the applicant’s residual liberty interest (if sufficient to trigger habeas corpus review) was a substantively reasonable decision made in a procedurally fair manner and therefore “lawful”. The respondent, in my opinion, has satisfactorily explained themselves. A habeas corpus order is denied.

 

IV.  Costs

 

[64]      Habeas corpus is a civil remedy. The normal rule in civil matters is that costs follow the cause, i.e. the result. The applicant has been unsuccessful in his claim. Although the respondent has not specifically sought costs, the respondent has not explicitly waived right to them either.

 

[65]      I note that my colleague Riordon J. has awarded costs against unsuccessful inmates in similar cases (See: Samms v. Atlantic Institution 2004 NBQB 140 ($750.00); Bird v. National Parole Board 2007 NBQB 96 ($950.00) and Cain v. Canada (Attorney General) 2011 NBQB 47 ($1,000.00)). I see no reason not to follow that practice. This approach is seen elsewhere as well (e.g. Robinson v. A.G. of Canada 2013 ONSC 7992 ($2,000)). I have in mind the policy considerations that animate the law of costs:

 

… aside from the purpose of providing the successful party with indemnification, awards of costs have been used as tool to influence the way in which the parties conduct themselves and to prevent abuse of the Court’s process.

 

(Doucet v. Spielo Manufacturing Inc. 2011 NBCA 44 at para. 117)

 

[66]      As Mission Institution v. Khela, supra, foretells and the present case exemplifies, it can be reasonably expected that many more challenges to prison administrative decisions will be brought by inmates to provincial superior courts given our greater accessibility and the now expanded scope of review by habeas corpus. Courts in locales where prisons exist are particularly on the front line. Unchecked, there would be no disincentive for inmates to seek to have a judge review the reasonableness of each and every administrative decision potentially impacting on a residual liberty interest, regardless of merit. Put differently, what would a person have to lose?

 

[67]      The potential impact and the added expense that would have to be otherwise borne by taxpayers to meet each and every challenge are patent. This case, for example, took beyond a full day. The federal government lawyer representing the respondent was required to file a brief on law and a case book of authorities and to also supplement the record on hearing. She travelled from Nova Scotia to appear at the hearing. On the other side, the applicant conducted himself in a respectful manner and mounted thoughtful arguments.

 

[68]      This is all to say that every litigant in society invoking the civil law in seeking redress before the courts is expected to assess the monetary risks should they be unsuccessful. I do not see why an inmate should not also be expected to engage in some introspective consideration of the relative merits of a potential claim, i.e. engage in a “risk-reward” assessment. Inmates too must shoulder some responsibility for their decisions in these regards.

 

[69]      At the same time, costs cannot become a roadblock to accessing the writ of habeas corpus. After all, habeas corpus is the law’s oldest front line bastion of protection against “unlawful” loss of liberty; in modern times including the protection against “unlawful” reduction or restriction of residual liberty interests of inmates. Habeas corpus can be an efficacious remedy suited to respond to a multitude of situations. Money cannot be made to act as bar to its resort.

 

[70]      In the end, the just balance is found in imposing more modest cost awards relative to the length of the hearings, the conduct of the party and the merits of the claim than might otherwise be imposed, recognizing an inmate’s special situation. This would not be an exercise in futility either. The evidence discloses in this case that inmates do receive some, albeit limited, pay from the government for work and study inside and otherwise do receive allowances.

 

[71]      In my opinion, the historical and societal policy considerations that inform costs awards should not stop at the outside of prison walls. Indeed, this is reflected in the decisions previously cited of my colleague Riordon J., which approach, to reiterate, I intend to follow.

 

[72]      In the circumstances of this case the applicant shall pay one set of costs in the amount of $750.00 all inclusive forthwith payable to the respondent Warden (Atlantic Institution) for the benefit of the Receiver General of Canada.

[1] Although in the wider sense habeas corpus is also a form of “judicial review”, the term ‘judicial review’ is used here to denote applications for the other prerogative writs and other remedies reserved exclusively to the jurisdiction of the Federal Court over federal administrative decisions. This, however, is not to be confused with the relief of “certiorari in aid” a procedural remedy used to bring the record below before the Court, as distinct from the substantive writ of certiorari used to quash a decision below. “Certiorari in aid” is available in conjunction with a habeas corpus application. (See: Mission Institution v. Khela, supra at paras. 36-38).
