Brantford Chemicals Inc. v. Merck & Co., Inc. [Indexed as: Brantford Chemicals Inc. v. Merck & Co.] Patent Appeal Board and Commissioner of Patents M. Wilson

Heard: April 28 and 29, 2005 Decision rendered: September 1, 2005

APPLICATION under s. 65 of the Patent Act (Can.) requesting that the Commissioner of Patents grant the applicant a compulsory licence.

BY THE BOARD:—This decision deals with an application made under Section 65 of the Patent Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. P-4, by Brantford Chemicals Inc. of Brantford, Ontario (hereinafter Brantford) alleg­ing that there has been an abuse of the exclusive rights of Patent Number 1,275,349 owned by Merck & Co., Inc. of Rahway, New Jersey, U.S.A. (hereinafter Merck).

The application was filed on April 30, 1999 and instructions to serve the application on the patentee were issued on June 21, 1999. Those instructions required the patentee to file and serve a counter statement and also required the applicant to file and serve a reply to the counter statement. The application was served on Merck on July 6, 1999, a counter statement was filed by Merck on November 3, 1999 while Brantford filed its reply on February 4, 2000. Merck also filed a supplementary counter statement on October 1, 2004 and Brantford filed a supplementary reply on April 26, 2005 Pursuant to subsection 69(2) of the Patent Act, Merck requested that a hearing be convened to allow for the cross-examination and further examination of the persons who attested to the veracity of the application. Such a hearing was held on April 28 and 29, 2005 at which time Dr. Keshava Murthy, President of Brantford Chemicals Inc., Dr. David Coffm-Beach, former President of Torpharm Inc., Mr. Ivor Hughes, patent agent and Dr. Bernard Sherman, President of Apotex Inc., appeared for the Applicant. Mr. Edward W. Murray, in-house Counsel of Merck & Co., appeared for the Patentee.

Brantford Chemicals Inc. was founded in 1974 and is a wholly-Canadian owned corporation engaged in the manufacture of fine chemical products, including chemical compounds intended for use in the manufacture of pharmaceutical products.

Enalapril and its acid addition salts are known to be useful as angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors for the treatment of hypertension. Basic salts of enalapril are also believed to be potentially useful as ACE inhibitors.

In 1996, Brantford discovered a substance known as sodium enalapril-sodium iodide complex (SESIC) and a method of making SESIC in pure form. They also found that SESIC could be de-com-plexed to produce pure sodium enalapril (SE). This is the subject matter of Canadian patent application 2,182,258, filed by Brantford on July 29, 1996. This application has subsequently been issued to patent on April 13, 2001.

Brantford wishes to manufacture SESIC and SE made from the complex for sale in Canada and for export. One of Brantford's pro­posed customers is Torpharm Inc., a Canadian corporation related to Brantford, which is involved in the manufacture of Pharmaceuticals in final dosage forms e.g. tablets and capsules. Merck & Co. was organized in the United States in 1891 and is (as of November 1999) the largest pharmaceutical company in the world with world-wide pharmaceutical sales of approximately $16 billion USD.

Merck & Co. is the owners of Canadian patent 1,275,349 which was issued on October 16, 1990 from an application which was filed on September 16, 1986. This application was divided from an appli­cation which was filed on December 6, 1979, claiming priority from a United States patent application which was filed on December 11, 1978. The title of the patent is AMINO ACID DERIVATIVES and it is directed towards a family of compounds which find utility in the treatment of hypertension in humans. Included in this family of com­pounds is enalapril and its salts. Since enalapril does not crystallize out of solution, it is converted into a salt to facilitate the formulation of tablets and the like. The claims of Canadian patent 1,275,349 encompass inter alia enalapril and its pharmaceutically acceptable salts as well as pharmaceutical compositions containing them. In July 1987, Merck's Canadian licencee, Merck Frosst Canada Inc. (now Merck Frosst Canada and Co.) was issued a Notice of Compliance (NOC) by Health Canada in respect of the VASOTEC® brand of enalapril tablets which are formulated from enalapril maleate and other ingredients. Immediately following receipt of the NOC, Merck Frosst began to produce tablets in Kirkland, Quebec and to market VASOTEC® brand of enalapril based tablets in Canada. These tablets are only available through a doctor's pre-scription. Merck Frosst obtains the required supply of bulk enalapril maleate from Merck & Co. or from one of its associated companies. VASOTEC® brand tablets compete in the market place with a number of other ACE inhibitors, as well as other products which are used to treat hypertension and congestive heart failure. However, this product has been a leading prescription drug in Canada in the treatment of hypertension and congestive heart failure, with annual sales in Canada in the order of $180,000,000.

On April 16, 1998, Dr. David Coffin-Beach, who was then President of Torpharm Inc., sent a letter to Brantford Chemicals Inc. In that letter, Torpharm stated that it was desirous of purchasing from 1,000 to 5,000 kilograms of SESIC for use in the manufacture of pharmaceutical products and enquired if Brantford was in a posi­tion to sell SESIC to Torpharm "given the Merck patent" and on what terms (appendix 3 of the Application). On that same day, Dr. Coffin-Beach also sent a letter to Merck & Co. In that letter, he stated that Torpharm wanted to obtain from 1,000 to 5,000 kilograms of SESIC and asked if Merck & Co. was prepared to sell it to Torpharm, and if so, under what terms (appen-dix 9 of the Application).

On April 17, 1998, Dr. Keshava Murthy, who was then General Manager of Brantford Chemicals Inc., wrote to Merck. In that letter, Dr. Murthy stated that Brantford had received a request to supply SESIC, but that Merck's patent appeared to claim an invention which embraced that compound. As a result, Brantford requested a non-exclusive licence which would permit it to manufacture SESIC and to sell it for use in the manufacture and sale of finished dosage forms. The letter requested that Merck confirm that it was prepared to grant a licence and to provide Brantford with its proposed royalty rate. A reply was requested within 15 days. The letter was sent to Merck, care of Goudreau Gage Dubuc & Martineau Walker, who, at that time, were the representative for service of Merck & Co. with respect to the 1,275,349 patent (appendix 5 of the Application). On July 2, 1998, Merck replied to Brantford through Merck's Canadian patent counsel. In that letter, Merck sought information from Brantford which it indicated was necessary before considering the grant of a non-exclusive licence (appendix 6 of the Application). On that same day, Merck's patent counsel also sent a letter to Torpharm Inc. and stated that "Merck & Co. Inc. does not manufac­ture the SESIC, nor are they aware of anyone who does..." (appendix 10 of the Application). This letter was in reply to the April 16, 1998 letter from Dr. Coffin-Beach.

On July 14, 1998, Torpharm replied to Merck's letter of July 2, 1998. In this letter, Dr. Coffin-Beach informed Merck that because of a recent decision of the Supreme Court of Canada, Torpharm does not require a licence from Merck (appendix 10 of the Statutory declaration of Edward Murray).

On July 15, 1998, Brantford replied to Merck's request for infor­mation and again asked if Merck would grant a licence and on what terms (appendix 7 of the Application).

Brantford did not receive a reply to its letter of July 15, 1998 and on September 16, 1998 it filed an application under Section 65 of the Patent Act alleging that Merck was abusing its patent rights and requesting that the Commissioner of Patents grant a licence. On January 29, 1999, the Commissioner refused to entertain that appli­cation because he found no evidence to suggest that the demand for the patented article was not being met by the patentee and he was not satisfied that Merck had refused to grant a licence or licences on reasonable terms.

It appears that there was no contact between Brantford and Merck on the subject of Brantford acquiring a licence to manufacture SESIC after the Brantford letter of July 15, 1998.

On April 30, 1999 the instant application was filed In its application, Brantford states that it is prevented from mak­ing and selling SESIC and SE made from SESIC because several of the claims (claims 1, 3 and 8) of Canadian patent 1,275,349 encom­pass both SESIC and SE. Accordingly, unless it has a licence from Merck, it would be an infringement for Brantford to practice its invention to make and sell SESIC and SE made from SESIC. Brantford alleges that Merck has abused its exclusive rights under patent 1,275,349 pursuant to Section 65 of the Patent Act. If a case of abuse is established, one of the powers of the Commissioner is to order the grant of a licence on such terms that the Commissioner may think are expedient. This is the purpose of the instant applica-tion, since Brantford is seeking a licence to make SESIC; to sell SESIC for use in the production of pharmaceutical dosage forms, including the use in making SE for the production of pharmaceutical dosage forms; to use SESIC for the manufacture of SE; and to sell SE made from SESIC for use in the production of pharmaceutical dosage forms (paragraph 38 of the Application).

In its application, Brantford alleges two grounds of abuse; it alleges that the demand in Canada for SESIC and SE is not being met to an adequate extent and on reasonable terms (paragraph 65(2)(c) of the Patent Act); and that there has been a refusal by Merck to grant a licence on reasonable terms (paragraph 65(2)(d) of the Patent Act). I will consider each in turn.

Paragraph 65(2)(c) Paragraph 65(2)(c) of the Patent Act reads as follows: 65.(2) The exclusive rights under a patent shall be deemed to have been abused in any of the following circumstances:

(c) if the demand for the patented article in Canada is not being met to an adequate extent and on reasonable terms;

First it must be determined if there is a demand in Canada for the patented article. In its application, Brantford asserts:

27. The demand for SESIC and SE in Canada is not being met at all by reason of Merck's refusal to supply or to grant a licence to Brantford.

28. At present, SESIC and SE is not available at all in Canada by reason of the following:

(a) no person presently produces and sells SESIC and SE in Canada, including Merck;

(b) no person in Canada is presently able to sell SESIC and SE in Canada by reason of the fact that such person would require a licence from both Merck under the Merck patent and Brantford under its patent;

(c) Merck has not granted any licences under the Merck patent. There appears to be some disagreement between the parties as to the "patented article". It is Brantford's position that the patented articles are the chemical compounds SESIC and SE (paragraph 27 of the Application). It is Merck's position that the term "patented article" refers to that which is patented (paragraph 45 of the Counterstatement). Merck argues that that which is patented is a family of compounds including medicines comprised of salts of enalapril. Both parties agree that Merck is fully supplying the Canadian market for enalapril maleate through its VASOTEC® brand.

When I examine Canadian Patent 1,275,349, I note that the claims are directed to a family of compounds, including the compound enalapril and can encompass several "patented articles" e.g. the compound, its salts and pharmaceutical compositions . containing them. The present application, however, is focused on two chemical compounds SESIC and SE. It is not contested that both of these compounds are covered by the claims of Canadian Patent 1,275,349. Accordingly I consider that they are both patented articles for the purposes of this application. Because there are two patented articles mentioned in the Application, I will deal with the question of demand for each of them separately.

Demand for SESIC There is only one disclosed use for SESIC; it can be decomplexed to produce SE in pure form.

It is Brantford's contention that there is a demand in Canada for SESIC and that that demand is not being met to an adequate extent on reasonable terms. The correspondence between itself and Torpharm shows that there is a demand for SESIC in Canada (by Torpharm Inc.) and that this demand is not being met by the patentee, as was stated in Merck's letter of July 2, 1998 to Torpharm.

Merck counters by arguing that the only demand in Canada for SESIC is the self generated demand made by Brantford's sister company Torpharm Inc Accordingly, it argues that this demand is not the type of demand contemplated under Section 65 of the Patent Act. Section 65 contemplates a market demand arising in Canada in the broader sense and not the potential demand of one customer. In Re James Lomax Cathro (1934), 51 R.P.C. 75 at 82 to 83, Dr. M.F. Lindley, Comptroller-General had the following to say about demand: Mr. Lloyd Jacob argued further that the demand for the patented valves in this country is not being met because Mr. Cathro's own demand for patented valves of American type for re-sale to users in this country and in the Dominions is not being satisfied. Now while it may well be, as Mr. Trevor Watson agreed, that a demand for a patented article by export merchant and traders in this country is one that ought to be taken into account under paragraph (c), it seems to me that any such demand must be an actual one by a trader or traders in business here and not merely one which an applicant for a licence hopes and expects to be in a position to make if and when he obtains the licence and has commenced business

It also seems to me that the demand in Canada must be a general one in the sense that there is a market in Canada that demands the patented article and that this demand is not being met. It could well be that an applicant for compulsory licence would be the sole supplier of the article but there must be a more generalized market demand in Canada for the patented article than the demand which created by the Torpharm letter to Brantford. It cannot be an artificial demand created by one trader. Therefore, I conclude that there is no demand in Canada for SESIC which is the type of demand contemplated in Section 65.

Demand for SE A complete review of the Application does not reveal that there is any specific demand in Canada for SE. In its April 16, 1998 letter to Brantford, Dr. Coffm-Beach only mentions that Torpharm would like to purchase SESIC. In the Application, Brantford states that "if Brantford obtains a licence, Torpharm will in fact purchase SESIC or SE made from SESIC from Brantford...". (paragraph 22 of the Application).

Furthermore, Merck states that there is no evidence that Torpharm Inc. has a Notice of Compliance (see Patented Medicines (Notice of Compliance) Regulations, SOR/93-133) for SE so that no medicinal formulation containing SE may be sold in Canada by Torpharm. During the hearing, it became clear that Brantford planned to carry out all of the work required to gain regulatory approval to market SE after obtaining a licence from Merck. (Transcript, pages 115, 116, 184, 185) At the date of filing of this licence application, Merck also had a patent in the United States for the same invention, making it impos­sible for Torpharm to sell SE in that country. Also, no evidence was presented to show that either Brantford or Torpharm had taken any of the actions required to gain regulatory approval to sell SE in the United States. As such, it is clear that at the time of filing of the Application, there could be no sales of SE based medicines in Canada or in the United States.

Merck has presented considerable evidence that its VASOTEC® brand of enalapril based tablets competes in the marketplace with a number of other ACE inhibitors, as well as other products which are used to treat hypertension and congestive heart failure in other ways. It also asserted that the Canadian market for products which are used to treat these diseases is being fully met. Brantford did not dispute this claim. Instead, Brantford indicated that if granted a licence, it would market SE based tablets to a substantially different group of Canadian consumers and would not compete with Merck in the Canadian market. However, Brantford has not presented any evidence about the group of consumers to whom they expect to sell their product. This

would have to be a very large group of Canadians suffering from hypertension or congestive heart failure but who do not have access to presently available products. From the amount of SESIC that Torpharm wishes to purchase, it would appear that Brantford expects to find a market which is about the same size as the one Merck currently supplies.

It is very difficult to imagine that, in spite of the wide range of products currently available, Brantford and Torpharm will be able to establish a market of new customers which is about the same size as the existing market. I think it is much more likely that if a licence were to be granted, SE would enter into direct competition with the existing products, including Merck's VASOTEC® brand of enalapril based tablets.

Because it is not currently possible to sell SE in Canada or in the United States and the current Canadian market for enalapril based ACE inhibitors is being fully supplied, I must conclude that there is no demand, either current or anticipated, for SE in Canada. Therefore, I find no abuse under paragraph 65(2)(c). Paragraph 65(2)(d) Paragraph 65(2)(d) of the Patent Act reads as follows: 65.(2) The exclusive rights under a patent shall be deemed to have been abused in any of the following circumstances:

(d) if, by reason of the refusal of the patentee to grant a licence or licences on reasonable terms, the trade or industry of Canada or the trade of any person or class of persons trading in Canada, or the establishment of any new trade or industry in Canada, is prejudiced, and it is in the public interest that a licence or licences be granted;

There are three elements to this type of abuse; there must be a refusal to grant a licence on reasonable terms, there must be a preju­dice to trade or industry and it must be in the public interest that a licence be granted. In order for Brantford to show that Merck has abused its patent, all three elements must have been made out. I will consider each in turn. Refusal to Grant a Licence In order to determine whether there has been a refusal by Merck to grant a licence on reasonable term I have carefully examined the correspondence between the two parties and the resulting actions taken.

There is a preliminary issue in that Brantford addressed its letter of April 17, 1998 to the firm Goudreau Gage Dubuc & Martineau Walker in Montreal (hereinafter Goudreau) and not to Merck either in New Jersey, U.S.A. or through the firm Gowling Strathy & Henderson (now Gowling Lafleur Henderson LLP or Gowlings) who are Merck's Canadian patent counsel. Section 29 of the Patent Act requires a non resident applicant or patentee to appoint a person or firm having a Canadian address as representative for all purposes under the Act including the service of any proceedings under the Act. When Merck filed the patent application which resulted in patent 1,275,349, it nominated Goudreau as its representative for service for any proceedings under the Act. According to the records of the Patent Office, this nomination was revoked by Merck on September 24, 1999 when Gowlings was nominated in Goudreau's place. Since all of the correspondence concerning this application was dated prior to September 1999, Brantford committed no error in addressing and serving its correspondence on Goudreau.

Prior to the filing of this Application, there was a series of corre­spondence between Brantford and Merck; three letters dated April 17, 1998, July 2, 1998 and July 15, 1998. Brantford asked Merck for a licence to make, sell and use SESIC in the manufacture of finished dosage forms. Merck replied by posing ten questions to Brantford. Brantford replied on July 15, 1998 answering the ten questions and requesting an indication regarding a licence and its terms "within the next few days".

The next step taken by Brantford was to file an application for compulsory licence on September 16, 1998 (hereinafter the first application). This first application was refused on January 29, 1999, on the grounds that a case for relief, as required by subsection 68(2) of the Patent Act, had not been made. On the question of whether there had been a refusal by Merck to grant a licence or licences on reasonable terms, the Commissioner said: Brantford implies that Merck did not reply to its July 15 letter and states that this alleged failure to reply amounts to a refusal to grant a voluntary licence on reasonable terms or at all. However I am not in agreement with Brantford's submission in this regard. In the first place the time that elapsed from the July 15 letter is approximately two months, to my mind not giving Merck sufficient time for a considered response. Secondly, no terms or conditions of any proposed non-exclusive licence were set out in Brantford's letter and thirdly,

only a very short, time frame within which Merck was invited to reply was mentioned. Accordingly I am not satisfied on the evidence presented that Merck has refused to grant Brantford a licence or licences on reasonable terms.

This was followed by Brantford's letter of February 11, 1999 to the Commissioner requesting reconsideration of the January 29, 1999 decision and the Commissioner's reply dated February 23, 1999 indicting that he had no authority under the Patent Act to consider further evidence. This was followed by the filing of the instant application for compulsory licence on April 30, 1999.

In my view, the first letter of April 17, 1998 is merely a one page request for a bare licence, I find this the opening of negotiations for a possible licence. I find it reasonable of Merck to reply posing the type of questions it did. I further find that Brantford answered these questions promptly. I find that this response added a new dimension to the request since the initial request appeared limited to SESIC while the response mentioned supplying SE. Upon receipt of the July 15, 1998 letter, Merck would have been aware that Brantford's customers would be offered SESIC and/or SE in bulk chemical form. The answers also clarified that either SESIC or SE may be put into final dosage form. Brantford also provided some information regarding regulatory approval that would be required of its cus­tomers abroad, or of it in Canada. I therefore find that by the July 15, 1998 letter, Brantford had given Merck sufficient basic information to consider whether it could grant a voluntary licence. I note that the language of the Act requires a refusal of a licence on reasonable terms. The request in the letter of July 15, 1998 from Brantford did not offer terms for a possible licence. The onus is on the person requesting a voluntary licence to suggest terms and con-ditions. (See L.P.A. Plastics (1976) Ltd. v. Windsurfing International Inc. (1981), 59 C.P.R. (2d) 188 (Com'r. Pat.) at 199; and Torpharm Inc. a Merck & Co. (2000), 9 C.P.R. (4th) 520 (Com'r. Pat.) at 532). The only terms that were offered appear in the first application for compulsory licence filed with the Patent Office on September 16, 1998. The Commissioner did not direct that this first application be served on Merck (see subsection 69(2) of the Patent Act) so that the terms set out in paragraphs 30 and 32 thereof were not communi­cated to Merck by Brantford. These same terms appear in paragraphs 38 and 40 of the instant application which was served on Merck on

July 6, 1999. I find this to be the date that terms were communicated to Merck. I now turn to the question of whether Brantford gave Merck suf­ficient time to consider the request. I have already determined that the letter of April 17, 1998 was merely a one page request for a bare licence without proposing terms (see L.P.A. Plastics (1976) Ltd. v Windsurfing International Inc. (1981), 59 C.P.R. (2d) 188 (Com'r. Pat.) at 199) and as such cannot be considered as a starting point for considering a licence. However, I do find that the second letter of July 15, 1998 does offer more information (but no terms) and gives a time limit of "within the next few days" in which to respond.

The evidence is that there has been no correspondence related to the grant of a voluntary licence between Brantford and Merck since July 15, 1998. I accept Merck's argument that the filing of the first application for a compulsory licence put a halt to any correspon­dence regarding a request made by Brantford for a voluntary licence. It therefore follows that the time period in the instant application is the identical time period presented for the first application for com­pulsory licence which was refused by the Commissioner of Patents on January 29, 1999 as being too short.

On balance, I am not satisfied that Merck has refused to grant a licence to Brantford. It is unreasonable to expect Merck to continue discussions regarding a voluntary licence when Brantford has alleged that Merck is abusing its patent rights by refusing to grant a voluntary licence. The time frame for Merck to consider a voluntary licence is identical to that in the first application which was refused by the Commissioner. No terms were communicated to Merck until July 6, 1999, the date that the instant application for compulsory licence was served on Merck. For these reasons I do not find that Merck has refused to grant a licence or licences on reasonable terms to Brantford. This is sufficient to dispose of any allegation made under para­graph 65(2)(d) of the Patent Act, nevertheless, I will consider the other elements under this paragraph. Prejudice to trade or industry It is Brantford's position that the fine chemical industry of Canada, particularly Brantford itself is being prejudiced in that the manufacture of SESIC and SE made from SESIC in Canada, and by

Brantford in particular is being prevented. Further, the pharmaceuti­cal dosage form manufacturing industry of Canada, and in particular that of Torpharm is being prejudiced in that the manufacture of dosage forms containing SESIC and SE made therefrom is being prevented. Finally it is alleged that the establishment of a new fine chemical trade and pharmaceutical dosage form trade is being prej­udiced in that the establishment of manufacturing and sale of SESIC and SE made from SESIC and dosage forms made therefrom is being prevented (paragraph 35 of the Application).

Brantford alleges that the failure by Merck to grant a licence will make Brantford unable to manufacture and sell SESIC and SE made from SESIC and will ultimately diminish its business and that of Torpharm Inc. Jobs for Canadians will be lost. The healthcare system of Canada will be prejudiced in that Torpharm and others are prevented from making and selling dosage forms containing SESIC and/or SE which would provide alternatives to the treatment of hypertension and lower costs to Canadian consumers through price competition. Paragraph 65(2)(d) includes three specific activities that could suffer prejudice; the trade or industry of Canada; the trade of any person or class of persons trading in Canada; and the establishment of a new trade or industry in Canada.

The Concise Oxford Dictionary, Eleventh Edition, Oxford University Press, 2004 defines the word "prejudice" as: n 1 preconceived opinion that is not based on reason or actual experience. 2 chiefly Law harm or injury that results or may result from some action or judgement.

v 1 give rise to prejudice in (someone); make biased. 2 chiefly Law cause harm to (state of affairs).

In considering paragraph 65(2)(d) of the Canadian Patent Act it is helpful to note the similarity between it and the language of the United Kingdom statute. Subsection 27(2)(d) of the Patents and Designs Acts, 1907 to 1919 (U.K), 9 & 10 Geo. V, c. 80, read as follows: (d) If by reason of the refusal of the patentee to grant a licence on reasonable terms the trade or industry of the United Kingdom or the trade of any person or class of persons trading in the United Kingdom, or the estab­lishment of a new trade or industry in the United Kingdom is prejudiced, and it is in the public interest that a licence or licences should be granted.

The phrase "trade or industry" has been considered by the courts in Re Robin Electric Lamp Co. (1915), 32 R.P.C. 202 at 213. Warrington J. in construing the equivalent provisions in the United Kingdom statute, stated

Again, the expression "trade or industry" seems to me to be used in a wide sense just as we speak of the cotton trade, or industry, and the woollen trade or industry; so that it is not enough to establish that a particular trader is unfairly prejudiced, it must be proved that the trade or industry as a whole is thus affected.

This construction was approved of in Re Brownie Wireless Co. of Gt. Britain Ltd. (1929), 46 R.P.C. 457 at 473, and James Lomax Cathro supra at 83. In applying this principle to the instant applica-tion, I find that Brantford has not adduced any evidence to conclude that the fine chemical industry of Canada or the pharmaceutical dosage form manufacturing industry of Canada, as a whole has been prejudiced. Therefore I do not find that the "trade or industry of Canada" has been prejudiced. This subsection was also considered in the Brownie case (supra at page 473) where Luxmoore J. in analyzing the language said:

The next phrase is "The trade of any person or class of persons trading in the United Kingdom." This is, in my judgment, not capable of so wide an inter­pretation as the preceding phrase, for while the word "trade" itself is of general import, the words that follow, "of any person or class of persons trading in the United Kingdom," especially when read with the phrase immediately follow-ing, must of necessity limit such generality to the existing trade of some person or class of persons. In view of the decision in the Robin Electric Lamp Company's case, and the fact that these words were inserted after (and I think because of) that decision, I am of the opinion that the phrase "the trade of a person" must be construed as referring to the existing trade of the applicant.

This principle was also set out by Fox Canadian Patent Law and Practice 4th Edition 1969 Carswell, Toronto at page 554-555 :

The term "trade of any person or class of persons trading in Canada" is not capable of so wide an interpretation as the preceding phrase and must be construed as referring to the existing trade of the applicant. This particular provision can, consequently, be of no benefit to an applicant who wishes to found a new business or to extend an old one.

The facts in the instant case are such that Brantford proposes to manufacture SESIC and SE from SESIC and sell either the SESIC and/or the SE for formulation into final dosage form by its sister company Torpharm Inc. The evidence is such that Brantford has no

existing trade in SESIC or SE or the manufacture of SE from SESIC in Canada. Furthermore no sales of, bulk fine chemical of the fmal dosage form are currently possible in the absence of regulatory approval. Accordingly, I find that what Brantford proposes is some­thing in the future. It can either be characterized as a new business for Brantford or an extension of an old one.

In my view it cannot be said that Brantford or a person in the same class of business as Brantford, is prejudiced by reason of the refusal of Merck to grant a licence or licences on reasonable terms.

The other inference from the evidence is that Brantford proposes to export SESIC and/or SE as a fme chemical. This endeavour is contrary to the concept "trading in Canada". Furthermore this is something that might happen in the future because Brantford in their application state that such exports would only be to approved desti­nations and there are none on the evidence. Therefore I am not satisfied that Brantford has demonstrated a prejudice to the trade of any person or class of persons trading in Canada. Throughout these proceedings, Brantford has indicated that it operates in the fme chemical industry or the fine pharmaceutical chemical industry (Transcript, page 62). There was some evidence that the pharmaceutical fine chemical industry is quite small in Canada, but that it is a viable industry, with Brantford being the largest participant (Transcript, page 63). However, there was also evidence that there are at least two other Canadian companies oper­ating in that precise industry. There was also evidence that Merck's enalapril based ACE inhibitor faces competition from a number of other ACE inhibitors, as well as other products which are prescribed to lower blood pressure. As a result, I have concluded that what Brantford proposes is not the establishment of a new trade or indus­try in Canada but the establishment of a new business in an existing trade or industry. This type of activity was dealt with in James Lomax Cathro supra at 84:

..." the establishment of any new trade or industry in the United Kingdom" has to be distinguished from the entry of a fresh trader into an existing trade or industry (see the Brownie case at 473 and the Robin Electric Lamp case at page 216) and all the Applicant here proposes to do is, in my view, to enter, for the purposes of manufacturing American-style thermionic valves, the trade or industry of the making of thermionic valves or screen grid thermionic valves, just as in the Robin Electric Lamp case the Applicant in that case with his

double-filament lamp was merely proposing to enter the trade of the making of tungsten filament electric lamps. In short, paragraph (d) does not appear to extend to the case of prejudice resulting merely to the establishment of a new business in an existing trade or industry.

Therefore, I am not satisfied that Brantford has proved that the estab­lishment of any new trade or industry in Canada has been prejudiced.

Public Interest "Public interest" is not defined in the Patent Act or in the Patent Rules, SOR/96-423, and there is very little Canadian jurisprudence on the meaning of the term. However, what jurisprudence there is suggests that the meaning to be attributed to those words, or words having the same general meaning, should be determined by "refer-ence to the context and the objects and purposes" of the relevant statute (see, for example, Memorial Gardens Assn. (Can.) Ltd. v Colwood Cemetery Co., [1958] S.C.R. 353). In Black's Law Dictionary, Revised Fourth Edition, 1968, public interest is defined as:

something in which the public, the community at large, has some pecuniary interest, or some interest by which their legal rights or liabilities are affected. It does not mean anything so narrow as mere curiosity, or as interests of the particular localities, which may be affected by the matters in question.

Therefore, I feel that the inclusion of the words "public interest" in paragraph 65(2)(d) requires me to assess the various competing interests, i.e. those interests which would favour the grant of a licence, as well as those that would oppose such a grant.

Brantford addressed the question of public interest in paragraphs 36 and 37 of the Application, which reads as follows:

36. Also by reason of Merck's refusal aforesaid: (a) The inability of Brantford to manufacture and sell SESIC and SE made from SESIC will ultimately diminish its business and that of Torpharm;

(b) Jobs for Canadians will be lost; and (c) The healthcare system of Canada will be prejudiced in that Torpharm and others are being prevented from making and selling dosage forms containing SESIC and/or SE made therefrom, which dosage forms would provide alternatives for the treatment of hypertension and lower costs to Canadian consumers, including provincial governments, through price competition.

37. By all of the foregoing, it is in the public interest that a licence be granted to Brantford.

In paragraphs 52 of Brantford's Reply to the Counterstatement, it argues "...As for Canada, the very issue at stake is whether Merck is

abusing its patent rights...", while at paragraph 54, it relies on the following passage from International Cone Co. v. Cons. Wafer Co., [1926] 2 D.L.R. 1015 (Can. Ex. Ct.) at 1019; affirmed, [1927] 1 D.L.R. 402 (S.C.C.): The respondent cannot hold its patent for the sole purpose of blocking trade; it must sell or grant a licence on reasonable terms. While the object and spirit of the Patent Act is to give a monopoly, yet the statute provides also a remedy to overcome any abuse of such monopoly. The statute provides measures to put a stop to any act which would work as a restraint on business, or which would be incompatible with the best interests of trade and commerce.

The opening sentence of this passage recognizes that blocking of trade cannot be the sole purpose of a patent. Paragraph 65(2)(d) requires me to consider the concept of the public interest in the con­text of prejudice.

The interests of Brantford are quite clear. If granted a licence, it would be able to manufacture and market SESIC and SE made from SESIC once other regulatory approvals have been obtained. Thus, Brantford might be able to obtain some market share for its enalapril based ACE inhibitor before the expiry of the Merck patent.

However, I do not believe that the refusal of a licence would cause any serious damage to the overall business of Brantford as an on-going business concern. There was a considerable amount of evi­dence presented to me to show that Brantford is a well established company with a large work force and significant annual sales.

It must also be remembered that the patent in question will expire in a little more than two years (October 2007). Brantford has not obtained approval to market SE as an ACE inhibitor in Canada. Evidence presented at the hearing indicated that there were no plans to do any of the work required to obtain approval until a licence was granted. While it seemed that there would be no difficulties in obtaining approval for marketing in Canada, the regulatory process can be time consuming. Dr. Sherman indicated that it could take up to a year (Transcript, page 170). As a result, there would only be a very short period of time in which SE could be on the Canadian mar­ket before the patent expired and the licence would become unnecessary. Taking these facts into consideration, the refusal of a licence would appear to have only a limited impact on Brantford.

Now, looking at the interests of Merck, I arrive at a similar con-clusion. There would be minor negative impact on Merck & Co. and its Canadian licencee, Merck Frosst Canada if a licence were to be granted for the same reason that there would be a minor positive impact on Brantford. Brantford would take some of the market for enalapril based medicines from VASOTEC® brand of enalapril tablets. Any jobs that were created by Brantford to manufacture SESIC and SE would probably be at least partially off-set by job losses in the manufacturing of enalapril maleate tablets. In order to fully consider the interests of the Canadian public, it is necessary to understand the rational for having a patent system in Canada. Parliament has recognized that there is a public interest in granting patents which give patentees the exclusive right, privilege and liberty of making, constructing and using the invention and sell­ing it to others to be used (Section 42 of the Patent Act). The patent system encourages research and innovation and public disclosure of the best way of putting an invention into use. The value of the patent system is described in Fox (Supra pages 1 and 2):

At no time in English history were all monopolies regarded as anathema either by the common law or by Parliament. If they were for the good of the realm their propriety was never questioned; indeed, if the grant covered a new trade or article it was not considered to be a monopoly. From this theory evolved the principle that a new invention or the setting up of a new manufacture was a laudable thing and that a grant of exclusive privilege to its introducer by an exercise of the Crown prerogative was a beneficial act that did not offend against the principle of the common law which regarded as illegal and in restraint of trade only those monopolies that took away any freedom or liberty enjoyed before the grant.

If a licence were to be granted, it appears that Canadians would have access to another ACE inhibitor at a lower price than Merck's VASOTEC® brand of enalapril tablets.

Both parties agreed that Merck & Co., though its licencee, has been supplying the Canadian market with enalapril based medica­tions since it was issued a Notice of Compliance by Health Canada in 1987. No evidence has been presented that there ever was a short­age of supply of enalapril based medicines in Canada. There was no evidence presented that the product which Brantford proposes to market is more effective and there was no evidence that VASOTEC® brand of enalapril tablets are being sold at an excessive price. In reviewing all of the facts of this Application, I have concluded that Merck has been exercising its patent rights in a proper manner.

Therefore, from all the evidence before me, I have concluded that the only benefit which the public of Canada would derive would be to have available another enalapril based product at a lower price for a very short period of time before the patent expired.

On the other hand, the grant of a licence would have the negative effect of undermining the patent system to some extent. In E. C. Walker & Sons Ltd. v Lever Bias Machine Corp. (1953), 20 C.P.R. 61 at 66, the Commissioner of Patents had the following comments: If I were to interpret the provisions of s. 65(2)(e) in the manner suggested by Walker & Sons, I am very much afraid that we would establish a practice whereby no one holding a patent could ever be sure that he is not abusing his rights by refusing a licence to a competitor and this especially in the case of a patent for a process or one for a machine to make a known product. The owner of a particularly successful patent could work his patent and sell the product thereof cheaper than his competitor and in such case the competitor would be able to prove prejudice to his trade and obtain a licence. The position of an exclusive licencee would be impossible and the effect of such a licence would be nullified. Competitors would wait until a product was well established on the market and then they would secure a compulsory licence and reap the benefit of any pioneering work done by others.

The public interest, in my view, is far better served by maintaining a strong, reliable patent system than by granting a compulsory licence to the Applicant in this situation As a result, I find that Brantford has failed to establish that Merck has abused its patent rights under Section 65 of the Patent Act. I therefore refuse this application for a compulsory licence.
